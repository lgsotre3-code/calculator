FHA vs. Conventional Loans: Which Is Right for You?

August 15, 2026 · 7 min read

When you start shopping for a mortgage, one of the first decisions you will face is whether to take an FHA loan or a conventional loan. Both can help you buy a home, but they work differently when it comes to down payment, credit score requirements and mortgage insurance. Here is what you need to know to pick the right one for your profile.

What Is an FHA Loan?

An FHA loan is a mortgage insured by the Federal Housing Administration. Because the government backs the loan, lenders can offer it to buyers who have lower credit scores or smaller down payments. The trade-off is that FHA loans require mortgage insurance premiums (MIP), both an upfront fee and an annual premium, for most of the life of the loan.

  • Down payment as low as 3.5% with a credit score of 580 or higher
  • Buyers with scores between 500 and 579 can qualify with 10% down
  • Upfront MIP of 1.75% of the loan amount, plus annual MIP
  • More forgiving on higher debt-to-income ratios
  • Loan limits are set by county and are generally lower than conventional limits

What Is a Conventional Loan?

A conventional loan is a mortgage that is not insured by the government. It is held or guaranteed by private companies such as Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac when it fits within conforming limits. Conventional loans usually demand stronger credit, but they offer more flexibility and can be cheaper overall for well-qualified borrowers.

  • Down payment as low as 3% through Conventional 97 programs for eligible buyers
  • Credit score of 620 or higher is typical
  • Private mortgage insurance (PMI) is required only when your down payment is under 20%
  • PMI can usually be removed once you reach 20% equity
  • Higher loan limits and more options, including jumbo loans

Which One Should You Choose?

An FHA loan is usually the better fit if your credit score is below 620, if you have a small down payment, or if your debt-to-income ratio is on the higher side. The lower down payment and more flexible underwriting can make the difference between buying now and waiting.

A conventional loan usually makes sense if your credit score is 620 or higher, you can put 3% to 20% down, and you want the option to drop mortgage insurance once you build equity. For strong borrowers, conventional loans often end up with a lower total cost over time, especially for larger loan amounts.

The deciding factor is rarely the loan type alone. Compare your actual offers: rate, closing costs and monthly payment with insurance included. Run your numbers through a mortgage calculator to see the real monthly difference between the two.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the minimum down payment for an FHA loan?

FHA loans require a 3.5% down payment when your credit score is 580 or higher. Buyers with scores between 500 and 579 may qualify with a 10% down payment.

Can I get a conventional loan with 3% down?

Yes. Conventional 97 programs let eligible first-time buyers put down as little as 3%, though you generally need a credit score of at least 620 and private mortgage insurance.

Is FHA mortgage insurance permanent?

In most cases FHA annual mortgage insurance lasts for the life of the loan. On loans with at least 10% down, it drops off after 11 years. Conventional PMI, by contrast, can usually be removed once you reach 20% home equity.

MIP vs. PMI: The Insurance Math That Decides Most Comparisons

Rate sheets get all the attention, but in FHA-vs-conventional decisions the mortgage insurance structure usually moves total cost more than a 0.25% rate difference does. Here is exactly how the two systems bill you.

How FHA MIP is built

  • Upfront premium: 1.75% of the loan amount, added to the balance at closing. On a $337,750 base loan (3.5% down on $350,000), that is ~$5,911 of extra debt from day one.
  • Annual premium: 0.45%–1.05% of the remaining balance depending on LTV and term — for this loan, roughly $260/month at first.
  • Duration: with under 10% down, annual MIP lasts the life of the loan. There is no cancellation trigger short of refinancing or paying off.

How conventional PMI compares

PMI has no upfront charge; it bills monthly (or as single-premium/lender-paid variants) and prices off credit score and LTV. For a 680-score borrower at 90% LTV, expect roughly 0.5%–0.9%/year. Critically, PMI dies: request cancellation at 20% equity or let automatic termination handle it at 78% original LTV.

The five-year cost table

$350,000 purchase, comparing 3.5%-down FHA against 5%-down conventional at representative 2026 pricing:

  • FHA: $5,911 upfront MIP (financed) + ~$260/mo × 60 = ≈ $21,500 of insurance cost over five years
  • Conventional: $0 upfront + ~$205/mo × 60 = ≈ $12,300, falling as balance amortizes — and cancelable around year 4–5 if appreciation plus paydown reach 20% equity

That ~$9,000 gap is why the conventional route wins whenever the credit score and down payment qualify. The FHA structure instead rewards buyers who could not otherwise close: flexible 580/3.5% and 500/10% score floors, DTI allowances above what conventional AUS accepts, and often sharper rates for thin-file borrowers.

The escape hatch most FHA buyers should plan on

A standard move is FHA now, refinance to conventional once equity crosses 20% — through appreciation, amortization, or both. If values rise 4%/year on the example home, that threshold arrives near month 40–48. From then on the conventional loan sheds its insurance while the FHA alternative would still be billing MIP in year 25.
