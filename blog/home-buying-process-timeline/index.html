Home Buying Process Timeline: From Pre-Approval to Closing

August 23, 2026 · 10 min read

Buying a home is one of the biggest financial decisions you will ever make, and it involves far more steps than simply finding a house you like and handing over money. Between your first serious conversation about buying and the moment you pick up the keys, you will pass through credit checks, pre-approval, house tours, negotiations, inspections, appraisals, underwriting, and a mountain of paperwork.

That may sound overwhelming, but here is the good news: the process follows a predictable sequence. Buyers who understand the timeline ahead of time make better decisions, set realistic expectations, and experience far less stress than those who improvise. In this guide, we break down every phase of the home buying journey — how long each stage typically takes, what happens during it, and what you need to do to keep things moving. Most buyers complete the entire journey in four to six months, though cash buyers can move much faster.

Phase 1: Financial Preparation (1–3 Months)

Everything starts long before you tour a single open house. Lenders will judge your finances with cold precision, so the stronger your foundation, the better your rate and the smoother your approval. This phase typically takes one to three months, depending on your starting point.

Check Your Credit

Pull your credit reports from all three bureaus (you can do this free at AnnualCreditReport.com) and review them for errors. A score of 740 or higher unlocks the best interest rates; conventional loans generally require at least 620, while FHA loans accept scores as low as 580 with a 3.5% down payment. If your score needs work, pay down revolving balances, dispute inaccuracies, and avoid opening new credit accounts. Raising a score meaningfully usually takes 30 to 90 days of consistent behavior.

Save for the Down Payment and Closing Costs

Decide on your target down payment. Twenty percent avoids private mortgage insurance (PMI), but many loans allow far less — FHA requires just 3.5%, and conventional loans go as low as 3% for first-time buyers. Beyond the down payment, budget an additional 2% to 5% of the purchase price for closing costs. On a $400,000 home, that means $8,000 to $20,000 on top of your down payment.

Reduce Debt and Stabilize Your Profile

Lenders look at your debt-to-income ratio (DTI). Most want your total monthly debts, including the future mortgage payment, below 43% to 45% of gross income. Paying off a car loan or a credit card balance before applying can make the difference between approval and denial.

Get Pre-Approved

The final step of this phase is mortgage pre-approval. A lender reviews your income documents, tax returns, bank statements, and credit, then issues a letter stating how much they are willing to lend. Pre-approval is not a guarantee of final financing, but it tells you exactly what you can afford and shows sellers you are a serious buyer. In competitive markets, offers without a pre-approval letter are often discarded immediately. Shop at least three lenders within a short window — multiple mortgage inquiries within 14 to 45 days count as a single inquiry for scoring purposes.

Phase 2: House Hunting (1–3 Months)

With a pre-approval letter in hand, the fun begins. House hunting takes most buyers one to three months, though hot markets can compress this into weeks and slow markets can stretch it longer.

Define Your Budget and Needs

Before touring homes, separate your wants from your needs. How many bedrooms and bathrooms do you truly require? Which neighborhoods work for your commute, schools, and lifestyle? Remember that just because a lender approves you for $500,000 does not mean a $500,000 payment fits comfortably in your life. Work With a Real Estate Agent

A buyer's agent brings market knowledge, access to listings (including some not yet on public portals), negotiation skill, and help coordinating every professional in the transaction. Interview two or three agents, ask about their experience in your target area, and choose someone who communicates the way you prefer. In most cases, the seller pays the commission, though the rules around this have become more negotiable in recent years — clarify compensation upfront.

Tour Homes and Make Offers

Most buyers see somewhere between 10 and 20 homes before finding "the one." When you find it, your agent helps you craft a competitive offer covering price, earnest money deposit (typically 1% to 3%), contingencies for inspection, financing, and appraisal, plus your proposed closing date. The seller may accept, reject, or counter. Expect at least one round of negotiation. Once both sides sign, the home goes under contract, and the clock starts ticking on the shortest — but most intense — phase of the journey.

Phase 3: Under Contract (30–45 Days)

This is where the paperwork engine really spins up. From accepted offer to closing day, plan for roughly 30 to 45 days. Here is everything happening simultaneously:

Home Inspection (Days 1–10)

Hire a licensed inspector immediately — most contracts give you only a limited window (often 7 to 10 days) to complete inspections. A general inspection costs $300 to $600 and examines the roof, foundation, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and more. If significant problems surface, you can negotiate repairs, request a credit, or walk away under your inspection contingency. Specialized inspections (radon, pest, sewer scope, mold) add cost but can save you from disasters.

Appraisal (Weeks 2–4)

Your lender orders an independent appraisal to confirm the home is worth what you agreed to pay. If the appraisal comes in at or above the contract price, you proceed. If it comes in low, you must renegotiate with the seller, make up the difference in cash, or contest the valuation. Appraisal turnaround varies widely by market — anywhere from a few days to three weeks.

Title Search and Title Insurance

A title company examines public records to confirm the seller legally owns the home and that no liens, judgments, or ownership disputes attach to the property. Most issues are routine (an old lien that was paid but never recorded), but genuine defects must be resolved before closing. You will also purchase lender's title insurance, and most buyers add an owner's policy for a few hundred dollars more.

Loan Processing and Underwriting

In parallel, your lender's processing team collects and verifies everything: recent pay stubs, W-2s or tax returns, bank statements, employment verification, and explanations for any unusual deposits. An underwriter then reviews the full package against lending guidelines. Respond to document requests within 24 hours — slow responses are the number one self-inflicted cause of closing delays. Once underwriting is satisfied, you receive clear to close, the magical phrase meaning your loan is fully approved.

Bind Homeowners Insurance

Lenders require proof of homeowners insurance before funding. Get quotes from several carriers, choose coverage that at least equals your replacement cost, and provide the binder to your lender at least a week before closing.

Phase 4: Closing Day

Closing day is the finish line, and it arrives faster than you expect.

Final Walkthrough (24 Hours Before)

Within a day of closing, you revisit the property to confirm it is in the agreed-upon condition: negotiated repairs were completed, no new damage occurred, and included appliances and fixtures remain. Bring your contract checklist. If something is wrong, closing can be delayed or funds escrowed until resolved.

Review the Closing Disclosure

By law, your lender must deliver the Closing Disclosure (CD) at least three business days before closing. Compare it line-by-line with your Loan Estimate. The three-day window exists specifically so you can catch errors — wrong fees, incorrect rates, or missing credits — without derailing the appointment.

Sign, Fund, and Get the Keys

The signing appointment itself takes one to two hours. You (and likely the seller, separately) sign the promissory note, deed of trust or mortgage, closing statement, and dozens of disclosures. You bring a cashier's check or initiate a wire for your down payment and closing costs — always verify wire instructions by phone using a known number, because wire fraud targets homebuyers relentlessly. Once documents are signed, the lender funds the loan, the county records the deed, and the home is legally yours. In some states recording happens the same day; in others, keys transfer the following morning.

Phase 5: After Closing — The First Few Weeks

Getting keys is not quite the end. Handle these tasks promptly to start homeownership on solid footing:

  • Set up utilities. Transfer electricity, gas, water, internet, and trash service into your name effective closing day. Some providers take days to activate, so schedule ahead.
  • Change your address. File with USPS, update your driver's license, banks, employer, insurance, and subscriptions.
  • Note your first mortgage payment date. Payments typically begin on the first of the month following your first full month — if you closed August 15, your first payment is usually October 1, not September 1. Confirm with your lender and set up autopay to protect your credit.
  • Store your documents safely. Keep your Closing Disclosure, note, deed, and insurance policy permanently — you will need them for taxes and any future sale or refinance.
  • Build a maintenance habit. Budget roughly 1% of the home's value annually for upkeep. Change HVAC filters, test smoke detectors, locate the main water shutoff, and consider a basic home warranty during year one.

What Can Delay the Process

Even organized buyers hit snags. Knowing the usual suspects lets you prevent many of them outright:

  • Inspection surprises. Major findings — foundation cracks, roof failure, active leaks — trigger renegotiation rounds that can add one to two weeks. Worse outcomes kill deals entirely, sending you back to Phase 2.
  • Low appraisal. When the appraised value falls below the contract price, someone must bridge the gap. Renegotiations, value disputes, or second appraisals commonly delay closing by a week or more.
  • Title problems. Unpaid contractor liens, old judgments, boundary disputes, or heirship complications can take anywhere from days to months to cure. A thorough title search early in the contract period surfaces these sooner.
  • Unmet loan conditions. Underwriters frequently issue conditional approvals requiring extra documents — a letter explaining a bank deposit, updated pay stub, or proof that a disputed charge was resolved. Slow responses snowball fast.
  • Buyer financing falls through. Buying a car, opening a credit card, changing jobs, or making large unexplained deposits after pre-approval can void final approval. Rule of thumb: between contract and closing, your financial life stays frozen. No new debt, no job changes, no big cash moves without telling your lender.
  • Seller-side delays. The seller misses their own closing deadline on the home they are buying, drags out repairs, or fails to clear a lien on time.

The best defense is responsiveness: return lender calls same-day, keep digital copies of every document, and build a one-week buffer into any moving plans tied to a specific closing date.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to buy a house from start to finish?

For most buyers, the entire process takes about four to six months. Financial preparation and house hunting typically take two to six months combined, and once you are under contract, closing usually takes another 30 to 45 days. Cash buyers can close in as little as one to two weeks because they skip loan processing entirely.

Does a mortgage pre-approval hurt my credit score?

A pre-approval requires a hard credit inquiry, which can lower your score by a few points temporarily. However, credit scoring models treat multiple mortgage inquiries within a 14-to-45-day window as a single event, so shopping several lenders over a short period has minimal impact. Checking your own credit never affects your score.

What happens between going under contract and closing?

During the 30-to-45-day contract period, the home is inspected, appraised, and searched for title defects. Your lender processes your full application, verifies your income and assets, orders the appraisal, and issues final loan approval. You also shop for homeowners insurance and schedule a final walkthrough before signing.

What can delay or derail a home purchase?

The most common delays are inspection repair negotiations, low appraisals that require renegotiation, title defects such as unpaid liens, missing loan documentation, last-minute changes in the buyer's financial situation, and appraisal or underwriting backlogs at busy lenders. Responding quickly to lender requests is the best way to stay on schedule.
